# Research notes: what makes recipe math hard

Collected 2026-11-04 before designing v2. Sources are at the bottom. Most of them are teaching resources, extension handouts, and cooking sites. I didn't find peer-reviewed studies of high-school recipe reading specifically, so treat these as practitioner evidence.

## What students get wrong when reading recipes

- **They skim.** They jump straight into cooking, skip measurements and "obvious" steps, and ignore yield and serving size. ([Food Science Toolbox](https://foodsciencetoolbox.substack.com/p/how-to-teach-middle-school-students), [Culinary Arts Curriculum](https://blog.culinaryartscurriculum.com/reading-a-recipe-lesson/))
- **They mix up tsp and Tbsp** (t vs. T). Recipes, equipment, and labels spell units differently: t, tsp, tsp.; T, Tbs, Tbsp, tbsp; c, C; oz vs. fl oz. ([Food Science Toolbox](https://foodsciencetoolbox.substack.com/p/how-to-teach-middle-school-students))
- **Fractions are the wall.** Teachers report that students don't grasp multiplying fractions when adjusting recipes. ([FamilyConsumerSciences.com](https://www.familyconsumersciences.com/2015/08/kitchen-measuring-skills-adjusting-recipes-activities/))
- For students with dyscalculia, following recipes is one of the everyday tasks that is hardest. ([Cleveland Clinic](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/diseases/23949-dyscalculia))

## What home cooks complain about and ask

- **"How do I halve 3 eggs?"** Eggs can't be split cleanly. Cooks round, or beat the egg and use about 1½ Tbsp for half a large egg. ([Food52](https://food52.com/hotline/3650), [RecipeLion](https://www.recipelion.com/Cooking-Tips/How-to-Halve-an-Egg-for-Baking/amp))
- **"How do I measure 1/3 of 1/3 cup?"** The answer has to be a combination of tools in the drawer, e.g. ⅓ cup ÷ 2 = 2 Tbsp + 2 tsp. Extension handouts publish exactly these "half and third" charts. ([UKY "Master Measurer"](https://fcs.mgcafe.uky.edu/sites/fcs.mgcafe.uky.edu/files/recipemath-mastermeasurer_0.pdf), [UNL "Reducing the size of recipes"](https://food.unl.edu/sites/unl.edu.ianr.extension.food/files/articles/documents/reducing-the-size-of-recipes.pdf))
- **Not everything scales.** Leavening, salt, and spices need judgment when scaling up. Common advice is to use less than the linear amount and taste. Bake time and pan temperature don't scale with the batch. Cooks also forget to scale the garnish or sauce. ([Joe Pastry](https://joepastry.com/?p=12735), [CIA Foodies](https://www.ciafoodies.com/culinary-math-recipe-conversion-factor))
- **Dry vs. liquid cups.** Both hold the same volume, but liquids go in spouted cups read at eye level, and dry goods are spooned and leveled in nested cups. It matters most in baking. ([America's Test Kitchen](https://www.americastestkitchen.com/articles/5516-do-you-really-have-to-use-different-measuring-cups-for-liquid-and-dry-ingredients))
- **fl oz vs. oz.** Fluid ounces measure space, ounces measure weight. A cup of water weighs about 8 oz, but a cup of flour weighs about 4½ oz.

## Learning science that shapes the game

- **Desirable difficulties.** Retrieval (recalling, not re-reading), spacing, and interleaving (mixing problem types) feel harder but stick longer. Interleaving forces the student to decide *what kind* of problem it is. That's exactly the "same conversion over and over" complaint, solved. ([APS: Desirable Difficulties](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/observer/desirable-difficulties))
- **Hints get gamed.** In tutoring systems, students click straight through hint ladders to the "bottom-out" hint that gives the answer. Warning students about how many hints remain *increased* bottom-out use in one experiment. Costing hints, and making the last rung a worked *example* rather than the answer, helps. ([LearnLab: Help abuse](https://learnlab.org/research/wiki/index.php/Help_abuse), [Shih, Koedinger & Scheines 2008](https://www.cmu.edu/dietrich/philosophy/docs/scheines/Shih_Koedinger_Scheines_2008_EDM.pdf))
- **Timers are contested.** Boaler argues timed tests trigger math anxiety. Other researchers say the evidence is weak and that time pressure helps fluency with facts already learned. A reasonable design: no clock while learning a skill, and an optional clock for fluency once the skill is known. ([Stanford/Boaler](https://ed.stanford.edu/news/boaler-timed-tests-and-development-math-anxiety), [Hechinger Report](https://hechingerreport.org/?p=93534), [Filling the Pail critique](https://fillingthepail.substack.com/p/timed-tests-and-maths-anxiety))

## Design takeaways

1. Answers should be **tool combinations** in whole numbers (1/4 cup + 1 T + 1 t), not "5 1/3 Tbsp".
2. Use **realistic amounts**: what recipes actually call for, in their real spellings (t, T, c, tsp., Tbsp).
3. **Mix the problem types** in every round: reading, choosing the tool, converting, scaling, and spotting a mistake.
4. **Work from recipe cards.** The goal is converting a whole recipe, mise en place style.
5. **Liquid vs. dry vs. weight** is a skill of its own: choosing the right tool.
6. **Judgment calls**, such as eggs, leavening, and pan size, as an advanced topic.
7. **Hints are questions**, and each costs something. The last rung is a similar worked example, not the answer.
8. The **timer** should be forgiving or optional, so speed is a reward and not a gate.
